  makes it into the framework.

Dario Amodei's plan for slowing AI development down doesn't open with a moratorium or a regulator. It opens with a job: an independent reviewer, sitting inside the company that builds the model, with real standing to hold a release back before it ships. Strip away the swarm-of-agents framing and the talk of international coordination, and what's left is a position I recognize, because a much smaller version of it is mine. Every knowledge source that goes into an agent I help scope gets curated by hand before a researcher ever sees it, specifically so the agent can't answer questions it has no business answering. Somebody has to be the one who says no. I already am that somebody, just at a scale nobody is writing a three-step plan about.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to overstate the resemblance. What Amodei is describing is a system with the theoretical capacity to act on the open internet at a scale that could cause damage nobody could contain after the fact. What I do is scope a chatbot so it doesn't misstate a submission requirement to someone whose study depends on getting it right. Those aren't the same category of risk, and treating them as though they were would flatten something that deserves to stay serious. A hospital's research arm isn't building anything with the reach to justify a comparison to an autonomous system operating past anyone's ability to pull the plug. I'm not claiming parity. I'm noticing a shape, not a scale.

The shape is this: caution has to be performed by someone, continuously, on requests that arrive faster than the caution can be explained. An independent reviewer inside a frontier lab doesn't get to make one ruling and move on. Every new capability, every expanded tool call, every request to widen what the model can reach, comes back across their desk again, and the job is to keep saying no to the version of the request that isn't safe yet, even when the version that is safe would ship faster without them. That's not a policy. It's a standing posture you hold against the pressure of everyone downstream who wants the thing to just work already. I know that posture because I hold it on a system with vastly lower stakes, and it still isn't free. It costs something every time, even at my scale.

What it costs doesn't show up in a proposal, because a proposal describes the reviewer's authority, not the reviewer's day. On my end, the cost lands on a researcher who asks the agent something reasonable, gets a version of "I can't help with that" because the answer sits just outside what I let the system draw on, and has to go find a person instead, on a system that was supposed to save them that exact trip. Nobody frames that as the caution working as intended. It reads as the tool falling short, and I'm usually the one who hears about it, because I'm the one who drew the boundary. If a frontier lab actually builds the review function Amodei is asking for, someone inside it is going to spend a meaningful share of their time being the person a frustrated team blames for a capability that technically exists but isn't allowed out yet. That person won't be in the announcement either.

I don't think that argues against the reviewer role. If anything, watching how often the smaller version of it gets treated as friction rather than as the actual function working tells me the frontier version will face the same pressure, at a scale where caving to it matters more. The people asking for the guardrail are rarely the ones who have to stand inside it on an ordinary request, absorbing the annoyance of someone who just wanted an answer and got a boundary instead. I already know what that job feels like from the inside. It isn't the headline. It's the part right after, where somebody has to hold the line on a request that seemed completely reasonable to the person making it, and explain, again, why the answer is still no.
