---
title: ✍️ The Badge Arrives Before the Hard Part Does
pubDate: '2026-10-02T21:14:15-04:00'
description: >-
  With the nonprofit behind the old November novel-writing challenge gone, three
  replacement platforms spent September rolling out their own versions, badges
  and sprint trackers included, and the gap between a word count and an actual
  draft is the part none of them are selling.
---
One of the platforms that picked up this year's big November novel-writing challenge walked me through its whole reward ladder before I'd finished reading what the organization even was: a badge at twenty-five thousand words, another at the finish line, daily sprints with other writers to keep the streak alive. Twenty-five thousand words is a real number. It took real hours to produce. It is not, in my experience, anywhere near the part of a draft where the actual problems show up.

The nonprofit that ran this challenge for over two decades folded last year, and this fall three different replacements are splitting what it left behind: a software company's branded version with its own prep month starting this week, a scrappier volunteer successor, and a worldbuilding site's take on the same thirty-day sprint. All three kept the same math the original used, fifty thousand words in thirty days, because the math is the one part of the format that never needed fixing. It's clean, it's trackable, and a dashboard can celebrate it in real time. None of that was ever the hard part of writing a novel. It was always just the part a tracker can see.

I'm about sixty thousand words into a first draft of my own, reading chapters aloud to a writing group as I go, so I have some standing on where the actual problems in a long draft tend to live. They don't live in the daily count. A few months back the back half of my book started moving noticeably faster than the front half, not because I'd decided to speed anything up, but because some unconscious part of me wanted to be done and started skipping the connective tissue a slower writer would have kept in. Nobody flags that for you by watching a number climb. Someone has to actually read the pages, weeks after you wrote them, and notice that a scene which should carry weight slid by in half a page. A badge has no mechanism for catching that, because catching it requires rereading, which is the opposite of the forward motion a sprint is built to reward.

The case for what these platforms are doing is stronger than I want to give it credit for, though, and it deserves a real hearing before I get to the part that bothers me. Most people who say they want to write a novel never produce a full draft of anything, and the reason is almost never that their fourth act sags. It's that they never sat down enough days in a row to find out what their fourth act even is. A public counter, a daily minimum, a crowd of strangers hitting the same milestones on the same calendar, that structure gets people past the only failure mode that kills a draft before it has problems worth having. Measuring raw output is a blunt tool, but it's aimed at the right target for a person who hasn't started yet. I don't think that's nothing, and I don't think it's dishonest of these platforms to lead with it.

Where it stops working is the moment a writer actually finishes the thirty days with fifty thousand words, because that's exactly the moment the real work starts, and none of the badges have anything left to say. A completed count tells you a person can generate language on a schedule. It can't tell you whether the plot holds, whether a morally compromised protagonist stays worth following to the end, whether a chapter you wrote confident at the time reads as confused to someone encountering it cold. Those answers only arrive later, usually from someone else's blunt reaction to pages you'd stopped being able to see clearly yourself. The software company running the largest of the three successors sells editing tools for exactly that gap, which tells you the gap is real even while the free challenge it's hosting keeps advertising the number that comes before it.

What stays with me isn't that the replacement platforms are wrong to lead with a streak. It's that the one piece of writing advice I'd actually trust came from someone in my group saying a scene felt rushed, out loud, after reading it slower than I'd written it. No tracker offered that. No badge was waiting on the other side of it. It just took someone willing to notice what the count couldn't.
