The screenshot going around this week isn't from a hospital. It's a settings page. A patient logs into their health records portal with a username and password, then sees a short list of categories, lab results, medications, allergies, immunizations, care plans, with a switch beside each one. Flip it on, and an AI assistant can read that category going forward. Flip it off, and it can't. The company behind the records platform is calling this patient-directed sharing, and describing it as letting a patient choose what the assistant sees. The login itself stays with the records platform, never passed to the assistant. Nothing shared this way gets used to train anything, according to the company. You can revoke it whenever you want. Read as one screenshot, this looks like the cleanest version of a decision that usually gets made for people instead of by them.

I've spent real time on a part of consent infrastructure that most patients, and most coverage of this kind of rollout, never see: the part where someone has to decide in advance what a checkbox actually grants, how long that grant holds, and what happens when the thing underneath it changes shape. Research consent in a regulated setting isn't a single toggle. It's a versioned document, re-signed when a study's scope changes, with a record of exactly which version a specific person agreed to and when, because a consent nobody can reconstruct later isn't one a regulator, or a participant, can actually rely on. A category checkbox labeled "medications" carries none of that. It's one bit, set once, standing in for a decision that in a research setting would need considerably more scaffolding than a settings page shows.

I want to give this rollout real credit before I get to where it worries me, because the design choices here are more careful than the reflexive privacy objection usually gives credit for. Keeping the portal login out of the AI company's hands entirely, rather than handing over a password it could reuse anywhere, is the right default, not a token gesture. Making the sharing opt-in, instead of a setting buried three menus deep and switched on by default, respects that most people haven't thought this through yet and shouldn't be assumed to want it. And the promise that nothing shared this way feeds back into model training is the one guarantee that actually answers the long-running fear people have about health data specifically: that today's convenience quietly becomes tomorrow's training corpus with no way to claw it back. None of that is nothing, and a lot of consumer software skips all three.

Where the design stops matching the stakes is in what the category toggle is actually standing in for. A real consent record in a regulated setting answers more than whether a party can see a category. It answers which specific items were disclosed, under what version of what agreement, as of what date, and what happens to that grant once the underlying data changes shape, say a new diagnosis lands in a chart that was shared under an older, broader-sounding label like "medical history." A switch on a settings page doesn't track any of that, because tracking it would mean building the kind of granular, auditable consent system that research teams build precisely because a yes to one version of a disclosure isn't automatically a yes to the next one. The gap between what the toggle promises and what it can account for later doesn't show up today. It shows up as a hole in somebody's reconstruction of events a year from now, when the question stops being "did they agree" and becomes "agree to exactly what, and as of which date."

There's a second thing a settings-page framing quietly drops, which is where the legal protection around that data goes once it's shared this way. The obligations that bind a records platform to specific handling and disclosure rules exist because the data sits inside a defined, regulated boundary. The moment a patient directs that same data outward, to a party sitting on the other side of that boundary, the protections built for the sending side don't automatically travel with it. That's not a defect specific to this rollout. It's a structural feature of how patient-directed sharing has worked for years, in paperwork long before it showed up in a toggle, and it's the one part a settings page, however carefully built, was never going to be the place to explain.
