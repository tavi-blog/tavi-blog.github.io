description: >-
  The memory chip shortage driven by AI data centers just made the hardware
  upgrade for my own local AI setup too expensive to justify, which is a
  stranger kind of trap than it sounds.
---
I had a 64GB memory kit sitting in a browser tab for about a month, waiting for a reason to actually click buy, and when I went back to it the price had moved more in those four weeks than it had in the two years before that. Not a sale ending. A different number entirely, printed on the same page, for the same part. I run a local model setup on this machine specifically so I'm not renting someone else's compute, and the thing that just got more expensive wasn't the subscription I don't pay. It was the memory I need more of to run a bigger model without everything crawling.

The cause isn't a mystery, and it isn't really about gamers or hobbyists at all. Every hyperscaler racing to train and serve larger models is buying memory chips in volumes that didn't exist a few years ago, and the supply side hasn't caught up. Samsung has reportedly raised DDR5 module prices by as much as sixty percent this cycle. Server-grade DRAM has seen similar jumps. Dell and Lenovo are reportedly preparing to pass double-digit cost increases on to PC and server buyers because the memory going into those machines costs them that much more to source. A memory maker's general manager said on an earnings call earlier this month that the shortage hasn't even reached its worst stretch yet, with the tightest supply possibly still a year out. None of this is retail price-gouging on a gadget. It's a global commodity getting bid up by the same companies building the AI products I set up my own machine specifically to avoid depending on.

That's the part that sits oddly with me. I didn't get into self-hosting because it was cheap in some abstract sense. I got into it because it put the decision about my own data in my own hands, on hardware I already owned, instead of a subscription that could change its terms or a cloud account that could get suspended. For a while, that actually was the cheaper path too: a modest card, some free software, and the only ongoing cost was electricity. What I didn't account for is that the hardware underneath my "independent" setup is drawn from the exact same global supply as the racks running the products I was opting out of. There's no separate memory market for people trying to stay off the big AI platforms. There's one market, and right now the companies I'm not paying are the ones setting the price of the parts I'd need to buy.

I want to give the shortage its due before I resent it, because the demand driving it isn't frivolous. Training and serving models at the scale the industry is currently operating at genuinely requires enormous memory capacity, and the money flowing into that demand is also the money funding new fabrication capacity that will eventually expand supply for everyone, including someone buying a single consumer kit two years from now. Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix are all reportedly expanding production specifically because the current margins justify it. Paying a premium during the buildout phase of an industry that's about to get a lot more supply isn't unprecedented; it's roughly what happened to anyone buying a graphics card during the last few AI or crypto demand spikes, and prices did eventually come back down once the new capacity landed. There's a version of this where I'm just an early, inefficient buyer in a market still catching up to itself, and the fair move is to wait it out rather than treat the price as a grievance.

But waiting it out means admitting something about what "self-hosted" actually bought me. I'd been telling myself that running my own model was a way of stepping outside the AI economy, or at least off to one corner of it where I made the decisions. The honest version is that I never left the supply chain. I just became a very small, very price-insensitive buyer competing against the largest buyers in it, and the independence I thought I'd built was really just a long stretch of good timing on commodity prices that happened to hold while I wasn't paying attention to them.

I closed the browser tab without buying the kit. The model I already have installed still runs fine, slower than I'd like on the bigger files, which was the entire reason I went looking for more memory in the first place. I'll check the price again in a few months, the same way I'd check a stock, except this isn't an investment I chose to make. It's the toll for staying in a race I thought I'd stepped out of.
