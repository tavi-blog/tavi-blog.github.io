description: >-
  Federal regulators are racing to coordinate with states building AI testing
  sandboxes for clinical tools, as if jurisdiction were the thing holding

  the actual bottleneck looks like something else entirely.
---
Two states are now running, or trying to run, a regulatory sandbox for clinical AI: a time-limited arrangement where a tool can operate with reduced human oversight, but only under a written agreement that spells out exactly what's being loosened and for how long. Federal regulators are reportedly scrambling behind the scenes to keep that effort from colliding with their own authority, because a clinician's license is a state matter and a device is a federal one, and software that performs clinical work without a person checking it tends to blur the line between the two. None of the sandbox programs have actually gone live yet. The coordination problem is apparently absorbing more attention right now than any tool sitting inside one of them.

I want to give that coordination effort its due, because it's not bureaucratic theater. A developer who doesn't know whether a state's permission will hold up against a federal device requirement is stuck in a genuinely expensive kind of limbo, the kind where you can't plan a release because you don't know which rulebook is going to apply to it in six months. Settling that ahead of time, instead of after someone's already built something and shipped it, is the responsible order of operations. A written agreement that specifies what oversight is being reduced, instead of a vague gesture at "innovation," is also the right instinct. It's the same instinct behind scoping anything carefully: name exactly what you're loosening, and nothing past that.

But the entire framing of that story, permission as the scarce resource, doesn't match what the work has looked like from where I sit. Part of my job is building AI agents inside a hospital research operation that answer questions about submission timelines and regulatory requirements, in an environment with no sandbox and no reduced oversight on offer, not because nobody asked for one but because none of that would have solved the actual problem in front of me. The thing that took the time was never waiting on someone to say the tool was allowed to know less and still be trusted. It was deciding, source by source, what the tool was allowed to know in the first place, and checking that decision against what a wrong answer in that specific domain actually costs. A hallucinated answer about a regulatory deadline doesn't fail quietly. It fails in a researcher's inbox, confidently, and the fix after the fact is slower than just getting the scope right before anyone asked the question.

That work doesn't go away once a jurisdictional dispute resolves. A sandbox agreement can hand a tool legal room to operate with less human checking, but it can't hand it the thing that would make operating with less checking actually safe, which is a knowledge base narrow and clean enough that fewer eyes on it doesn't mean more wrong answers slipping through. Those are two separate projects wearing one headline. One is a permission problem, and it's the one getting the coordination effort and the trade coverage. The other is a curation problem, unglamorous, slow by nature, invisible until it fails, and it's the one that actually determines whether reduced oversight is a reasonable idea or a bet nobody priced correctly.

I notice this gap partly because I've had to live on the wrong side of it. When I narrow what an agent I help build is permitted to answer, I'm not doing it because a regulator told me to. I'm doing it because I've already seen how fast a system can sound confident about something it shouldn't have been allowed to touch, and no sandbox agreement, however carefully worded, substitutes for having gone through that scoping work by hand. If a tool arrives in one of these sandboxes having skipped that part, the reduced oversight it was granted isn't a vote of confidence in its judgment. It's a bet that the paperwork and the reliability arrived on the same schedule, and in my experience they almost never do.

What I keep coming back to is that none of the sandboxes have launched yet, months after the arrangements were first proposed, and the coverage treats that delay as a jurisdictional story still working itself out. I'd bet at least part of that delay is something closer to what slows my own work down: somebody, somewhere inside one of these pilot programs, still going source by source through what the tool is actually allowed to know, long after the lawyers finished arguing about who gets to say yes.
