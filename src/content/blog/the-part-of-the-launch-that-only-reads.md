title: "\U0001F4E5 The Part of the Launch That Only Reads"
pubDate: '2026-11-11T20:19:21-05:00'
description: >-
  A new healthcare AI product can draft a prior authorization appeal and read a
  patient's full chart, but can't write anything back into the record itself,
  and coverage keeps calling that a trust problem the model will eventually
  outgrow.
---
A new healthcare AI product launched this week that can read a patient's chart across two of the biggest hospital record platforms in the country, assemble a prior authorization request, check it against an insurer's own coverage policy, and draft an appeal when a denial doesn't hold up. The launch customer list reads like a roster of major health systems and insurers. What it can't do, mentioned in almost every write-up as a footnote rather than the headline, is write anything back into either record system itself. Someone still has to take the draft and enter it by hand.

Most of the coverage treated that gap as a maturity question, the kind of caution a company extends before it trusts its own product all the way. The American Medical Association's numbers on prior authorization make it easy to see why the product exists at all: something like forty requests a week per physician, hours of staff time lost to them, whole positions at some practices that exist only to process them. A tool that can shoulder the reading and drafting part of that load is a real reduction in a genuinely miserable task, and I don't want to undersell that just because the next step is being withheld. Rolling out the read side first, watching it work across real institutions, and holding the write side back until it's earned is a reasonable sequence for anyone shipping software into a record a hospital's own compliance office answers for. I'd want it built in that order too.

Where I part from the "it'll catch up once it's trusted more" framing is that I don't think trust is actually the thing standing between the two. Reading a feed into a dashboard and writing a line back into the record a hospital submits to a regulator for are not the same category of request, no matter how good the system doing the reading gets. I've spent a long time on the side of this that pulls from half a dozen source systems to build the dashboards and automations a research team actually uses, and every one of those projects sorted itself into the same two bins almost immediately. Getting a read feed approved is a conversation about what data gets shared and under what agreement. Getting write access into the actual system of record is a different conversation entirely, because the moment a piece of software can author something in that record, it becomes part of the record, with its own audit trail and its own share of the liability every time it's wrong. None of the automations I've helped scope write into the system a compliance office would actually audit. That was never because the automations weren't accurate enough yet. It's that the approval for touching the record isn't graded on accuracy. It's graded on who gets to be an author of it.

A federal rule now sets deadlines for how fast insurers have to respond to prior authorization requests and requires an API for submitting them by next year. That rule is aimed at the insurer's side of the exchange, how fast the answer comes back, not at who's allowed to write into a hospital's own chart. It's easy to read the two timelines, the rule's deadline and the product's current read-only limit, as converging toward the same finish line, the point where regulation and capability both clear the way for full automation. I don't think they're running the same race. One is about how quickly a decision has to be returned. The other is about who's allowed to be the one who writes it down, and that question doesn't move just because the model underneath got better at reading.

What I expect, having watched this split hold in a much smaller version of the same system, is that the write-back gap outlasts the model's own improvement curve by a wide margin. The chart was never waiting on the AI to get good enough to be trusted with a pen. It was waiting on someone, somewhere in a compliance office, to decide that a vendor's software gets to be an author of a patient's record and not just a very capable reader of one, and that's a decision a product launch announcement doesn't get to make on its own, no matter how clean the draft it hands back looks.
