---
The Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense went live this week on the federal network that's supposed to let any connected hospital pull a patient's record from any other connected hospital without a phone call, a fax, or a CD mailed between two buildings. The announcement leaned on scale: tens of thousands of organizations now connected, billions of records exchanged since the network launched a few years ago, two of the largest health systems in the country finally talking to the rest of it. Read as a press release, it's a genuine milestone. The federal government spent years getting competing vendors and competing hospital systems to agree on a common way to ask for a record and get one back, and that's a real coordination problem solved at a scale most industries never attempt.

Read as someone who spends a chunk of most weeks pulling data out of more than one system to make a single number mean the same thing in both places, the announcement is answering a different question than the one that actually determines whether this helps anyone. The milestone counts records moved. It says nothing about whether the record that arrives matches anything already sitting in the system that received it. Those are not the same achievement, and the gap between them is the part of health data work that never makes it into a press release because it has no clean number attached to it.

I build and maintain the dashboards and automations a hospital research team relies on, which means I spend a fair amount of my job on exactly this seam. A study doesn't live in one system. It lives in pieces spread across several, and getting those pieces to agree with each other is most of the actual work, not a formatting afterthought at the end of it. A date field that looks identical in two systems can be stored two different ways underneath. A record that's supposed to be unique can show up twice because two different source systems each think they own the canonical copy. None of that is a failure of the network moving the data. The network can move a record perfectly and still hand you something that doesn't line up with what you already have, and the only way to find that out is to actually try to use it for something, which is precisely the step a transfer-volume milestone doesn't measure.

I don't think the people building this network are wrong to celebrate it, and I don't think the scale they've hit is a hollow number. Getting two federal health systems, each with its own decades of accumulated infrastructure, onto one shared exchange standard is a harder problem than it sounds, and the fact that it happened at all is worth taking seriously as progress. Plenty of integration efforts die in committee long before they reach a working pipe between two systems this size. The honest version of my skepticism isn't that the pipe doesn't matter. It's that the pipe was always the easier half of the problem, and the harder half, whether the thing coming through it means what the receiving system assumes it means, doesn't get solved by expanding who's connected to the network. It gets solved one join at a time, by someone checking whether a key actually matches before building anything on top of it, and that work scales roughly linearly with how much data moves, not downward the way a network effect is supposed to work.

What I expect from here isn't failure. It's a second wave of announcements, a year or two out, about data quality tooling bolted onto the exchange after someone downstream discovers that having access to a record and being able to trust it are different problems. That's not a criticism of the rollout. It's just the order these things always go in: connect first, because connection is the part that photographs well, and let the part that actually determines whether the data is usable surface later, quietly, in whatever team ends up stuck reconciling it. I've been that team, on a scale small enough that nobody writes a press release about it. The federal version of the same problem is just now arriving at the size where it finally will.
