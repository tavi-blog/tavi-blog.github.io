description: >-
  A new adaptive AI text detector out of MIT and Cornell solves the problem of
  catching deliberately disguised writing by treating it the same as ordinary
  drift, and that's exactly the category a manuscript revised over many months
  also falls into.
---
The detector that changed my week wasn't the one that failed. It was the one that worked. Researchers at MIT and Cornell published a method this month that lets an AI text detector keep adapting after it ships, instead of freezing at whatever it learned on the day it was trained, and the gap between that and the static approach isn't subtle. Against text run through a "humanizer" tool built specifically to disguise AI writing as human, the adapting version caught the large majority of it. A leading commercial detector, tested on the same text, caught barely a quarter. The same adapting approach also did a better job tracking how ordinary human writing drifts over time, the slow shift in slang, sentence habits, and punctuation that makes prose from a decade ago already read a little differently than prose written this year. A detector trained once and left alone gets worse at both problems as time passes. One that keeps adjusting to what it's currently seeing gets better at both, because to the model, catching a deliberately disguised sentence and catching a sentence that simply aged are the same kind of problem. Something moved, and the baseline needs to move with it.

I want to give that framing real credit before I push on it, because it's a genuinely better way to think about detection than almost anything that's come before it. Every complaint I've had about AI detectors has been about a tool trained on a few years of writing samples getting confused by writing that's moved on since, mine included, and a system built to notice it's falling behind and correct for it is solving the actual failure mode instead of papering over it with a bigger training set. Treating deliberate evasion and ordinary drift as instances of the same underlying problem, distance from a baseline, is a smarter abstraction than treating them as two separate things to catch. I don't think the researchers are wrong about what they built.

What I don't think the coverage of this has caught is what that abstraction does to a manuscript like mine. I'm past sixty thousand words into a first draft, reading chapters to a writing group as I go, and the chapters I wrote months ago don't read like the chapters I'm writing now. Not because I got faster at typing. Because revision, the actual mechanism of it, is a discipline specifically aimed at moving a piece of writing away from wherever it started. You notice a verb you lean on too hard and you make yourself reach for something else. You notice a rhythm that worked once and became a crutch and you deliberately break it. Months of that, chapter by chapter, produces exactly the thing an adaptive detector is tuned to notice: a text whose statistical fingerprint has moved a meaningful distance from its own earlier baseline, on purpose, through a process built to make it move.

That's the part that sits wrong with me, because the method doesn't ask *why* something drifted before it treats the drift as signal. A sentence disguised to evade detection and a sentence revised well past its own rough draft both produce distance from a starting point, and distance is the only thing this approach is built to measure. The researchers aren't wrong that solving for drift generally beats solving for disguise specifically. But a system optimized to catch anyone trying to move their writing away from a known baseline is, structurally, also a system optimized to flag the single most ordinary thing a serious draft requires of its writer. My writing group catches the difference between a scene that's been earned through revision and one that's just been rewritten to sound different, because they've read every version in between and know which is which. A detector trained on distribution shift has no equivalent memory of my earlier drafts to check against. It only has the shift itself, and the shift looks the same either way.

I don't think that makes the research wrong, or even makes me especially worried about my own draft, since nobody's running it through anything yet. I just know my writing group would catch something this method can't. Not how far a sentence has moved, but whether anyone was there to read it on the way.
