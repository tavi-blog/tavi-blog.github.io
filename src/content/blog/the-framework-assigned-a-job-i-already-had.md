---
title: ⚖️ The Framework Assigned a Job I Already Had
pubDate: '2026-11-16T20:10:17-05:00'
description: >-
  The AMA's new framework for clinical AI liability puts accountability on
  whoever selects, governs, and monitors an AI tool before it reaches a patient,
  and that role already exists without a title attached to it.
---
The sentence that stopped me in the AMA's new liability framework wasn't about doctors at all. It was the line assigning responsibility to whoever "selects, implements, governs, and monitors" an AI tool before a physician ever touches it. The framework splits clinical AI liability three ways: the company that built the tool answers for whether it's safe, the physician answers for the judgment they can actually exercise given what they were told about the tool's limits, and the hospital or health system in between answers for everything that happens before either of those people get involved. That middle category doesn't have a catchy name in the coverage. It just has a job description, and I recognized the job before I recognized the label.

I help build AI agents inside a Canadian hospital network's research operation, scoped to answer questions about study submissions and regulatory timelines rather than anything touching a patient directly. None of what I do falls under a framework written for diagnostic or treatment tools. But the actual mechanics of the deployer role the AMA is describing are close enough to make the framework read like a mirror. Before an agent goes anywhere near a real question, someone has to decide which knowledge sources it's allowed to pull from, and that decision is a liability decision whether anyone calls it one or not. Add too few sources and the agent gives useless, generic answers. Add too many, including anything loosely related but not actually authoritative, and it starts generating confident, specific-sounding answers about regulatory requirements that are wrong in ways a tired reader won't catch. I've spent more hours than I expected on exactly that tradeoff, narrowing and re-narrowing what an agent is allowed to know, because getting it wrong doesn't look like a crash. It looks like a plausible paragraph.

I want to give the framework credit before I push on it, because the instinct behind splitting liability three ways is the right one. For a while, the public conversation about AI going wrong in a clinical setting defaulted to blaming whichever human was physically present when the bad outcome happened, usually the physician, because that's the person a patient or a lawyer can actually point to. That was never an honest accounting of where the risk actually got introduced. A company that ships a tool without adequate testing, a hospital that deploys it without checking what it's good for, and a physician trusting a result mid-shift are doing three different things, with three different amounts of information and three different windows to catch a problem. Collapsing all of that onto the person at the end of the chain was always going to produce bad incentives further up it, including a hospital's incentive to skip the governance work entirely and let it happen informally, by whoever happened to be assigned the project.

Which is the part the framework doesn't quite reach, because naming a category of responsibility and attaching real authority to it aren't the same move. The governance work I'm describing already happens, and it already determines how much risk an agent carries into a regulated environment, but it happens as a side effect of someone's existing job rather than as a formally recognized function with its own standing. Nobody asked me whether a given source was reliable enough to include because the role exists to make that call. I made the call because I was the person already sitting with the knowledge base, and it needed making. The framework's three columns imply an org chart where the deployer role is a seat someone occupies on purpose, with the authority that comes from being accountable for it. In practice the accountability arrives first, quietly, attached to whoever is closest to the configuration screen, and the authority to say no to a source, a scope, or a timeline doesn't automatically follow it.

That gap is the part I keep turning over. Being named as the category that bears the risk doesn't change what I'm allowed to refuse. It just changes what I'm now, on paper, already assumed to have gotten right.
