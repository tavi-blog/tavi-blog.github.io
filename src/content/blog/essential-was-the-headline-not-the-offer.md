---
title: "\U0001F4CE Essential Was the Headline, Not the Offer"
pubDate: '2026-11-15T20:10:51-05:00'
description: >-
  A Canadian staffing survey calls contract workers essential and reports strong
  performance numbers, but hiring plans for them haven't moved in two years.
---
A survey released this week found that eighty percent of Canadian hiring managers whose companies use contract workers say those workers have, at some point, outperformed their full-time counterparts. The same survey found that half of Canadian companies now build contract labour into their staffing strategy on purpose, not as a stopgap, and that most hiring managers expect to keep doing it. Express Employment Professionals and Harris Poll ran the numbers and landed on a headline calling contract workers "essential" to Canadian business. Reading past the headline, the number that mattered more to me was a quieter one: planned hiring of contract and temporary workers for the rest of the year sits at roughly the same share it did last year, and the year before that. Essential, it turns out, doesn't come with a growth curve attached to it.

I want to take the caution seriously before I treat it as a contradiction, because flat hiring intentions aren't automatically a sign that contract work is undervalued. A lot of Canadian employers are navigating tariff disruption and general economic uncertainty right now, and in that climate, holding steady rather than expanding any category of hiring is a reasonable, even prudent, response. Some share of the workers inside that figure, roughly a tenth of the work at these companies by the surveyed managers' own estimate, are there because they chose a flexible arrangement over a permanent one, not because an employer forced it on them. None of that is invented. A company that isn't scaling up its use of contract labour isn't necessarily a company that has decided contract labour doesn't matter.

What doesn't sit right with me is the specific combination the survey hands us: a performance signal that's overwhelmingly positive, and a commitment signal that hasn't moved in two years. If contract work were simply a flexible staffing lever, I'd expect those two numbers to track each other, with usage expanding somewhere close to the rate that performance is being praised. They don't track. The performance finding is the kind of result a company would use to justify bringing people on permanently, if permanence were the actual destination of the arrangement. It isn't. The contract was never a trial period for something else. It's the destination, regardless of what the scorecard says once you're inside it.

I work on a contract inside a large hospital network, doing analytics and automation work that has grown considerably past what the title attached to the role would suggest. The usual answer to that gap, when it comes up at all, is a conversation about retitling the role, not about converting the contract. That isn't a complaint about my specific situation. It's the same shape the survey is describing from the outside: performance and scope can both expand while the structure around the role stays exactly where it started, because the structure was never the thing being evaluated in the first place. Nobody measures whether you were good enough to deserve permanence, because permanence isn't on offer as a reward for being good at the job. It's a separate decision, made for separate reasons, usually budgetary ones that have nothing to do with the quality of anyone's work.

That's also, I think, what the flat intention number is quietly telling us, more than the tariff story does. If economic caution were the real driver here, I'd expect the praise to soften along with it, employers saying less about how well contract workers perform while they pull back on using them. Instead the praise holds steady and the hiring plans hold steady too, just at a level well below what the praise would predict if performance were actually the thing being rewarded. That gap between what gets said about the work and what gets committed to the worker isn't new, and it isn't something a survey fixes by naming it more generously next year. It just means the people doing that tenth of the work probably shouldn't read "essential" as an offer.
