The headline that moved chip stocks hardest this week had nothing to do with chips. Nvidia's board forced out Jensen Huang days before the company's quarterly earnings, after a report characterizing one of his relationships as a possible target for recruitment by a foreign intelligence service, an allegation the company hasn't confirmed, on top of separate admitted affairs that had nothing to do with that story at all. The stock fell double digits on the resignation news alone, and the sell-off didn't stay inside one ticker. The whole semiconductor index posted one of its worst sessions in years, dragging down memory makers and equipment suppliers who had no exposure to Huang, no seat on his board, and no reason a lawyer would ever name them in whatever the investigation turns up.

I want to give that sell-off a fair hearing before I say the sector overreacted, because leadership risk at a company this size isn't nothing. Huang wasn't a figurehead CEO. He personally built the relationships with hyperscalers and foreign governments that determine who gets chip allocation and who gets an export license, and a scandal that touches national security optics, even loosely, raises the real odds that those relationships get harder to manage, at least for a while. None of that is manufactured concern. A company built this far around one person's personal credibility is exposed when that credibility takes a hit, and the market is allowed to price that in.

What it isn't supposed to do, or at least shouldn't be able to do without me noticing, is spread that exposure evenly across companies that share nothing but a sector label. A memory maker's backlog doesn't get shorter because a different company's CEO had an affair. A lithography supplier's order book doesn't care who sits on Nvidia's board. When the whole index drops by a similar amount regardless of which company actually carries the risk, that isn't the market pricing a specific exposure. That's attention moving as a herd and calling itself a repricing.

I hold semiconductor exposure, and for most of this year I've had a research tool half-scoped and still not built, meant to separate exactly these two kinds of information: the data that tells you something about whether actual demand for these chips is still growing, and the headline that tells you something about how a story is traveling through a newsroom. This week handed me the cleanest version of that test I could ask for, because the two inputs arrived days apart instead of blended together the way they usually do. First the scandal, days of it, then the quarter: revenue more than doubling year over year, data center revenue up well over a hundred percent, next-quarter guidance raised past what the Street had modeled, a bigger buyback approved on top of it. If the tool existed, this is the exact week it was scoped for, two cleanly separated signals instead of the usual mess.

It still doesn't exist, and I've mostly stopped pretending the gap is a technical problem. I don't need a dashboard to know which of those two inputs the thesis I hold actually depends on. I could tell you the guidance number without looking it up. What I can't claim is that my attention behaved like I understood that. For most of that week I checked the stock price the way everyone else did, for whatever filing or interview had leaked that day, and treated the earnings call almost as a formality to get through before the real story resumed. The print beat every number that mattered to the thesis, and it got a few hours of coverage sandwiched between days of someone else's marriage falling apart in public.

That's the part I can't build around. A tool can separate signal from noise on a spreadsheet. It can't make the signal interesting enough to compete with the noise for the only thing that was ever actually scarce here, which is how long I was willing to keep looking at it.
