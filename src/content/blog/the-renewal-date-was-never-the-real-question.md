---
title: ⏳ The Renewal Date Was Never the Real Question
pubDate: '2026-08-12T21:05:04-04:00'
description: >-
  Seventeen states are weighing bills to let gig-staffing apps classify nurses
  as 1099 contractors instead of employees. The fight over that classification
  skips the more interesting question: why an institution wants a workforce
  built this way at all, contract app or not.
---
Ohio's Senate passed a bill in June that hands "gig nursing" platforms a legal framework for treating the nurses they place as independent contractors rather than employees, and versions of the same bill are now moving through legislatures in more than a dozen other states. The pitch from the platforms is autonomy: pick your own shifts, name your own rate, work three hospitals in a week if you want to. The pushback, mostly from nursing unions, is that 1099 status quietly strips away minimum wage floors, overtime, workers' comp, and unemployment insurance from people doing bedside work in an understaffed ward. Both sides are arguing about the correct box to check on a tax form. Neither side is asking why a hospital wants a workforce it can staff this way in the first place, and that's the part I actually recognize.

I'll take the platforms' side of the argument as far as it deserves to go, because it deserves to go somewhere. Fixed scheduling in healthcare is genuinely brutal, and a nurse who wants to work nights three weeks a month and take the fourth off has real reasons for preferring a model that lets her do that over one that doesn't. Autonomy isn't a talking point invented by the apps; it's a thing shift workers have wanted for as long as shift work has existed, and pretending the appeal is pure corporate manipulation gives the people taking these gigs less credit than they deserve.

But autonomy is the individual-level story, and it's not the reason an institution signs the contract with the platform. I'm not a nurse and I don't work bedside, but I've spent my whole time at a large hospital network on a contract that gets renewed on a schedule I don't control, sitting next to permanent staff doing work that's functionally adjacent to mine. Nobody had to explain to me why that arrangement exists on the institution's side. A contract role is a cost that doesn't accrue the obligations a headcount does. It doesn't need a pension line, doesn't create the same severance liability, doesn't force a budget conversation when a program gets cut instead of a person getting laid off. The nurse booked through an app and the analyst on a renewable term are solving the same problem for the institution, just at different pay grades and with different degrees of press attention.

What that arrangement does to the person inside it is the part the legislative fight has no mechanism to capture, because it isn't a line item. When your position renews on someone else's clock, you calibrate what you're willing to invest in accordingly. You still do good work, because most people do regardless of their contract status, but the horizon shortens. You stop being the person who volunteers to fix the thing that will take eighteen months to pay off, not because you don't care whether it gets fixed, but because you might not be there to see it through, and pushing hard for a slow-moving change from a seat you don't know you'll hold in a year is a specific kind of exposure that permanent staff simply don't carry. Multiply that calculus across enough of a workforce and you get an institution that's very good at running the same processes it ran last year and slower at building anything that requires someone to stick around and champion it.

That's the trade the "gig nursing" bills never put a number on, because it isn't the kind of cost a fiscal note can capture. The platforms will tell legislators this expands the labor supply and solves a staffing shortage, and in the narrow, immediate sense they're right, a filled shift is a filled shift. What doesn't show up in that math is the difference between a floor that's staffed and a floor that's staffed by people who have a stake in whether it gets better. An institution can hit its coverage numbers entirely with workers who have no reason to advocate for anything beyond their own shift, and by every metric that shows up in a quarterly report, that will look like success.

I don't think the fix is banning contract labor in healthcare, mine included, since some of what makes it attractive to workers is real and worth keeping. But I'd take any of these bills more seriously if a single one of them asked an institution to account for what it loses when it optimizes a workforce for renewability instead of investment, because right now nobody's writing that question down, and the platforms are the only ones being asked to answer for it.
