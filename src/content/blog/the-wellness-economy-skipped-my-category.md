---
title: "\U0001F4C8 The Wellness Economy Skipped My Category"
pubDate: '2026-11-14T20:14:19-05:00'
description: >-
  A new wellness economy report splits spending into a premium longevity tier
  and a budget volume tier, and recomposition doesn't register in either one.
---
The number making the rounds this week is $7.5 trillion, what the Global Wellness Institute is calling the size of the wellness economy now, up close to nine percent from the year before. That number didn't hold my attention for long. What did was the shape underneath it: growth concentrated at two ends of the market and almost nothing in between. One end is described as "hard" wellness: longevity science, recovery diagnostics, wearables, AI coaching layered onto a premium club membership. The other end is "soft": walking groups, Pilates, the high-volume low-price gyms that have been quietly taking share for years. Both are reportedly thriving. The report frames it as a split market finding its two natural shapes.

I want to take that framing seriously before I push on it, because it isn't wrong about either end. The premium tier is selling something real: people who can afford a longevity panel and a coach who reads it are buying actual information they didn't have before, and some of that information changes what they do. The budget tier is solving a real problem too, maybe a more important one: a lot of people got priced or intimidated out of fitness entirely, and a cheap, low-friction gym that doesn't try to sell them a diagnostics package is the only version of this they were ever going to walk into. Neither end is a scam. A market finding two legible price points to grow around is not, by itself, evidence of anything broken.

What it does mean is that the thing I actually do doesn't live at either end, and the report has no way to see it. Training consistently, eating close to maintenance instead of cycling a surplus and a deficit, treating protein as the one number worth tracking closely and letting the rest stay loose, isn't a premium service and it isn't a budget one. It doesn't need a longevity panel to tell me whether it's working, and it doesn't need a ten-dollar class pass to make it accessible, because most of the cost was never the gym membership. It was consistency, which doesn't show up as a line item anywhere. A market report built from spending data can only describe the wellness economy that has a price tag attached to it. The part of this that actually works for most people going into it doesn't have one, so a report measuring an economy by what's being purchased will never find it, not because it's hiding, but because there was nothing there to buy.

That's worth sitting with rather than treating as a gotcha, because the report isn't pretending to be something it's not. It's a market sizing exercise, and recomposition was never going to be a market. I built a tracking app for exactly this reason: the practice generates data, protein hit, training consistency, roughly where calories landed, but almost none of that data has a transaction attached to it, which means it never shows up in anything that measures an "economy." The premium tier gets measured because people pay a company to measure it for them. The budget tier gets measured because the membership itself is the product. The middle gets measured by nobody, because the person doing it is both the customer and the entire supply chain, and there's no invoice generated at any point in that loop.

I don't think the polarization the report describes is the problem. Two legitimate price points coexisting is a perfectly normal way for a market to shake out, and plenty of people are genuinely better served at one end or the other than they would be in some imagined undifferentiated middle. The part that bothers me is narrower: a $7.5 trillion number gets treated as a measure of how the world is doing wellness-wise, when it's really just a measure of how much the world paid someone else to do it for them. The actual outcome, the thing closest to what "wellness" is supposed to mean, was priced at whatever a notebook and a kitchen scale cost, and a notebook and a kitchen scale will never be a growth sector. The report isn't lying. It's just counting receipts, and some of the most durable work doesn't leave one behind.
