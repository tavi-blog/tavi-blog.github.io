There's a boxy external enclosure sitting on my desk with a mid-range card in it, connected to a thin work laptop over a single Thunderbolt cable, and until recently the most interesting thing about that setup was that I could run spreadsheets on the same machine that plays a racing game at full settings an hour later. I bought the dock and the card used, separately, a while back, on the theory that I didn't want to own two computers when one, extended, could do both jobs. It was a consolidation choice. I didn't buy it to get ahead of anything.

This year it quietly turned into a hedge, and I didn't do anything to make that happen. Consumer memory and GPU prices have been climbing for months because AI data centers now account for something like seventy percent of global memory chip production, and the manufacturers who make that memory converted consumer-line fab capacity over to the high-bandwidth chips hyperscalers were signing multi-year contracts for. A memory kit that cost under a hundred dollars in early last year is going for several times that now. Laptop makers have confirmed price increases in the fifteen to twenty percent range across their lineups, more at the high end, and gaming handhelds that used to sit under a thousand dollars are launching closer to two. None of that is a rumor being traded on a forum. It's manufacturers publicly adjusting price sheets because the input costs actually moved.

The case for why this is happening is stronger than the framing of "gamers getting squeezed" usually allows, and it deserves to be taken on its own terms before I get to the part that bothers me. A fab line only has so much capacity, and a company selling that capacity is going to sell it to whoever pays the most for it. Hyperscalers are paying enormously more per chip than a consumer graphics card business ever generated, and reallocating scarce production toward the buyer offering the better return isn't corporate villainy, it's what a rational supplier does with a fixed resource. Nobody was owed cheap memory forever, and treating this purely as an injustice ignores that the alternative, capacity sitting idle instead of going to the higher bidder, isn't obviously the fairer outcome either.

What that framing leaves out is who gets to keep participating in PC gaming at all once the reallocation is finished, and that's not evenly distributed. I already owned a laptop, an enclosure, and a card from before the crunch really hit, which means the price of getting a gaming-capable setup for me was mostly sunk. Someone starting from nothing this year is pricing out a card, a chassis or dock, and enough memory to run either, at the top of a multi-year runup that analysts don't expect to ease until 2027 at the earliest. The path I took by accident, buy the components separately and attach them to hardware you already have instead of buying a purpose-built machine, is quietly becoming one of the few ways left to get in under the new price floor, and it's a path that specifically requires already owning something to attach the parts to. It's not a workaround anyone designed. It's just what's left standing once the obvious route gets expensive enough.

There's a small, telling sign that even manufacturers have noticed this. A budget laptop shipped late this month with external GPU support built in as a selling point, not a hobbyist afterthought, which is a tacit admission that the honest way to sell affordable performance right now is to stop trying to cram the expensive part inside the box and let people bolt it on separately, on their own timeline, when they can afford to. That's the exact bet I made for a completely unrelated reason, arrived at from the manufacturer's side instead of the buyer's, and I don't think it's a coincidence so much as the same constraint pushing two different people toward the same shape of answer.

I don't feel clever about any of this, mostly because I know the only reason I'm not the person priced out this year is that I made an unrelated decision on a different timeline, before the input costs that would have punished a later version of the same choice existed yet. The card in that enclosure is still just a card I bought because I didn't want a second computer. It only looks like foresight from here.
