The headline coming out of Tuesday's results was about a governor's race in a state that holds something like a quarter of the country's planned data center capacity, and by Wednesday morning the chip sector had priced that headline in hard. I hold semiconductor exposure in a registered account up here in Canada, so I read the move the way I read most of these: as a line on a statement that had nothing to do with anything I voted on, because I don't get a vote in any of it. What actually slowed me down wasn't the governor's race. It was a separate tally sitting a few paragraphs lower in the same coverage: forty-one local measures on data centers, spread across ten states, and voters said no to building more of them in all but one.

Most of those forty-one weren't anywhere near a swing state storyline. They were township zoning questions in Ohio, a rezoning vote in Wisconsin, a construction ban in a Florida beach town, the kind of ballot line that normally gets decided by people who show up to argue about a stop sign. Twenty-one of the forty-one were in Ohio alone. The margins in several of them weren't close: a data center ban passing by three to one in one town, a comparable spread in another a few miles over. An exit poll on the broader question found something like two-thirds of respondents didn't want one built near their own home, and that number didn't appear to split cleanly by which party they'd otherwise voted for.

I want to give the governor's-race read its due, because it's not a lazy one. That state really does hold an outsized share of planned buildout, and a chief executive who campaigned explicitly on slowing it down is a more direct lever on the sector's growth curve than any number of township votes could be on their own. One zoning measure in one Ohio village changes essentially nothing about a trillion-dollar capital expenditure cycle. Markets are supposed to weight the big, structural risk over the diffuse, local one, and on that logic the sector should trade on the state with the most gigawatts, not the state with the most ballot measures.

Where that read stops working for me is the word "diffuse." Forty-one is not one. A single zoning fight is noise; a near-identical result repeating across ten states, in towns that otherwise have nothing in common politically, stops being a local story and starts being a measurement of something the governor's race can't actually tell you: whether the public tolerance for this specific kind of construction, in this specific form, has quietly run out in advance of any party doing anything about it. A governor can be voted out in four years. A pattern where the ask every time is lower water use, lower power draw, and a say in where the thing gets sited doesn't go away on the same clock, and it doesn't care which party's governor is approving the permit.

That distinction matters more to someone holding the equity than it would to someone just watching the politics, because it changes what kind of risk this actually is. A single adverse election is a shock you can wait out. A standing floor of local resistance that shows up whenever a data center is actually proposed, regardless of who's in the state capitol, is a tax on sitting and timeline that doesn't reset when the news cycle moves on. I went looking for a dashboard or a tracker that would have shown me that pattern building before this week, something that aggregates these local measures the way earnings trackers aggregate guidance cuts, and found nothing built for it. The information was sitting in county clerk filings and local paper archives the whole time, in exactly the kind of boring civic record nobody builds a feed for, while the governor's race got a live leaderboard.

What I keep sitting with is how little my own account cares about the distinction I just made. It moves on the governor's race either way, because that's the number everyone else is trading on this week. The forty-one votes are the one I'd actually want priced in, and I have no way to buy or sell on them specifically, only to notice, from several provinces and one international border away, that a few thousand people in townships I couldn't find on a map without help just told an entire sector something it hadn't priced yet.
