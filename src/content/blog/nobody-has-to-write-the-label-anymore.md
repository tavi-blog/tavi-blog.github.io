  required it, I know exactly what disappears when nobody has to.
---
Buried about two-thirds of the way into a federal rule finalized last week, after several pages about which of sixty health IT certification criteria survive and which don't, is a single change that drops the "model card" requirement for predictive tools built into hospital software. The rule is being sold on the number attached to it: a little over a billion and a half dollars in compliance costs removed from the health IT certification program, framed as clearing the way for vendors to ship tools faster instead of documenting them. The specific thing getting cut is the requirement that a predictive decision-support feature disclose, in a standard format, what it was trained on, who it was validated against, and where it's known to be unreliable. The agency kept the newer category these tools fall under. It just stopped requiring the label that was supposed to come with it.

I understand the argument for cutting it, and I don't think it's a cover story for something worse. A billion and a half dollars is a real number, not a rounding error, and it's made of actual hours: vendor staff filling out standardized disclosure forms for incremental software updates, compliance teams reviewing those forms, all of it layered on top of a certification program that was already slow before anyone added an AI-specific requirement to it. The model card standard was also new enough that it hadn't built up much institutional muscle memory yet, which means a fair amount of what got cut was paperwork that existed on paper more than in practice. If the honest alternative was a formal disclosure requirement that vendors filled out performatively just to clear a checkbox, cutting it might not cost the industry the thing it's named after.

Here's where I stop agreeing, though, because I've written the actual document this requirement describes, for a model nobody made me document at all. A few years ago I built a predictive tool for work that estimates how long a clinical study submission will take to clear initial authorization, based on what's known about it the day it's filed. Nothing in my job required me to produce anything like a model card for it. I did it anyway, informally, because I knew the people who'd be looking at that number had no way to tell on their own what it was good for and what it wasn't. The model was trained on the kind of submission that showed up often enough to generalize from. A study with an unusual structure, something the historical data barely had examples of, was exactly the case where I'd trust the prediction least, and the only reason anyone using it ever knew that was because I'd written it down and said so, outside of any system that required me to.

Writing that document was itself part of the real work, the part that let someone other than me use the number safely. An unlabeled prediction keeps its blind spots exactly where they were, just invisible, and the person relying on it finds them the hard way, by getting burned once. I built mine small enough and informal enough that writing it down cost me an afternoon, not months. The institutions this rule is actually about are shipping predictive features into electronic health records used across hundreds of hospitals, at a scale where nobody downstream has any realistic way to reverse-engineer a model's blind spots through trial and error, the way I eventually could with a tool I built myself and watched closely.

The need for that kind of documentation survives this rule just fine. Only the requirement to produce it is gone, and that requirement was doing more work than its paperwork reputation suggested. A vendor that already takes this seriously will probably keep writing something like it, because good engineering teams tend to document their own blind spots whether or not a regulator demands it. A vendor under pressure to ship the next feature on schedule now has one fewer reason to spend the time, and the hospital buying that feature has one fewer way of knowing which kind of vendor it just bought from.

I keep thinking about the gap between the two versions of this I've lived. Mine was small enough that I could personally verify, case by case, when the model was starting to drift outside what it knew. Nobody building toward hundreds of hospitals gets that luxury, which is exactly why the document mattered more there than it ever did for me, and exactly why I don't expect many of them to keep writing it once nothing's checking.
